## What does it mean to dream of your childhood home?

You are back in the kitchen you grew up in. The light is right, the smell is
right, and you move through the house without thinking about where the rooms are.
Often you are an adult in the dream, and the house is the only thing that is old.

The childhood home is one of the places people return to most often at night, long
after the house has been sold or demolished. Nostalgia is rarely what drives it.

## The place the body remembers best

### Rooms you could walk in the dark

No building is stored in as much detail as the one you grew up in. It is where you
learned what a home is, and the dream therefore has a ready set of rooms to build
action in. Much of what happens there is borrowed scenery rather than message.

### The periods that call it up

The dream tends to come when something is being established anew: a move, a
separation, a new job, a first child. What you once learned at home about safety
and belonging becomes relevant again when you have to make the same thing yourself.

### When the house is wrong

Very often something is wrong with the house. A room is larger, a staircase is
missing, a door leads the wrong way. The discrepancy is usually more interesting
than the rest of the dream, and it is worth trying to remember exactly what was
different.

## What met you there

**If the house was empty:**
Can mirror a transition where you stand alone with something that used to be
someone else's responsibility.

**If the family was there as before:**
Often concerns a need for what was predictable, rather than missing the people
themselves.

**If someone who has died was present:**
Common, and not alarming. Dreams of [the deceased](/en/dreams/deceased-person/)
often use the house as their stage because that is where the memories lie thickest.

**If the house was derelict:**
Can point to something you have leaned on no longer holding, without that having
to concern the family.

**If you were not let in:**
Tends to point to a distance from where you come from, which can be both painful
and necessary.

## The house that no longer exists

Some dream the house as it was, others as it became after it changed hands. The
latter is often described as more unsettling, even though it is the more realistic
version. The feeling that a place has moved on without you is probably something
this dream is particularly good at carrying.

Many dream of a childhood home they no longer have access to. The dream then takes
on a double character: it gives back something that is gone, and it reminds you
that it is gone. Both can be felt at once.

The dream sits close to related dreams of [houses](/en/dreams/house/) in general
and of being a [child](/en/dreams/child/) again, but differs from them in that the
place is real and precise. It is not just any home. It is yours.

## Sources and further reading

- G. William Domhoff, *The Scientific Study of Dreams* (2003) — the continuity
  hypothesis, relevant to the childhood home presenting itself in periods when
  questions of belonging are live again.
- Calvin Hall and Robert Van de Castle, *The Content Analysis of Dreams* (1966) —
  normative data, relevant because it shows how much space familiar homes occupy
  among dream settings.
- Carl Gustav Jung, *Man and His Symbols* (1964) — on the house as an image of the
  psyche, relevant to why the rooms in the dream rarely match the real ones.
