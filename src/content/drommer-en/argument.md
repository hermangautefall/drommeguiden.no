## What does it mean to dream about having an argument?

An argument in a dream can leave you with an uncomfortable sense of conflict — especially if it was with someone you love. But an argument in a dream is rarely a prophecy or an accurate picture of a relationship. Far more often, it's an inner dialogue you're having with yourself.

## Who's really speaking in the argument

### An inner conflict, externalized

It's one of the dream's most-used devices: two sides of yourself in conflict, presented as two different people. The person you're arguing with in the dream often represents a part of you — maybe the responsible side against the spontaneous one, the self-sacrificing against the self-interested, what you want against what you think you should do.

Who you're arguing with — and what about — is the key. What does the other person represent for you?

### Unresolved tension in a real relationship

Sometimes the argument in the dream is more direct: there's something between you and that person that hasn't been said, something that's nagging, something unaddressed. The argument in the dream is then the psyche's dramatization of that tension — a safe arena to say what wasn't said.

The question becomes: what would you actually say, if you could?

### Suppressed anger finding its way out

Anger that's suppressed during the day tends to find its way into dreams at night. If you're in a situation where you can't show anger or frustration — for social, professional, or personal reasons — the argument in the dream is a release valve.

There's nothing dangerous about that. It's actually healthy. Anger that finds expression — even in the imagery of a dream — is anger being processed rather than left to accumulate.

### Boundaries being tested and asserted

To argue is to mark a boundary. The argument in your dream can mirror a need to set boundaries in real life — with a specific person, a situation, or an expectation you no longer want to live up to. The psyche rehearses boundary-setting in the dream's safe space.

## How the argument ended

If you argued with someone close to you, it's either an unresolved issue in that relationship, or the person represents a side of yourself. If you argued with a stranger, it's more often pure inner conflict — the stranger is an unknown part of yourself that you're at odds with. If the argument ended in reconciliation, an inner conflict is on its way to resolving, and something contradictory is starting to find peace. If it escalated and got worse instead, tension is rising in your life, and something needs addressing — either internally or in an actual relationship. If you said something you regretted, it's often because part of you is afraid of hurting others with your honesty or your anger. And if you won the argument, it can mirror a wish to be heard and acknowledged — or a feeling that something in you needs to "win."

## From Jung's complexes to the saga duel

Conflict is one of psychology's most-studied interpersonal phenomena — and one of the least studied in a dream context. That's surprising, because conflict imagery is extremely common in dreams.

Jung described what he called "complexes" — emotionally charged clusters of thought and feeling that can "take over" in dreams and act as independent figures. The person you argue with in a dream is often the activation of such a complex: a part of yourself carrying a heavy emotional charge.

In the Old Norse sagas, disputes over honor — the ritualized duel and the verbal contest — are among the most formalized patterns of conflict. What's striking is that the sagas' conflicts always concern something fundamental: honor, truth, belonging. The argument in a dream often carries the same gravity, even when it's about something trivial.

## Sources and further reading

- Carl Gustav Jung, *Man and His Symbols* (1964) — the complex: the charged knot in the psyche that the dream stages as a scene.
- Ernest Hartmann, *The Nature and Functions of Dreaming* (2011) — how suppressed anger takes shape in dream imagery.
- G. William Domhoff, *The Scientific Study of Dreams* (2003) — the continuity hypothesis, and why the argument in a dream is so often about yourself.
