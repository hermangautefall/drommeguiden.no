Almost everyone has a story like this — either their own, or from someone they trust. A dream that showed something that actually happened. A dream in which someone announced their own death, and then died. A dream about an accident, a phone call, a meeting — and then, days or weeks later, exactly that. Inexplicable. Eerie. Unforgettable.

Prophetic dreams are one of the most fascinating and contested phenomena in the psychology of dreaming. They're no longer dismissed as flatly as they once were. And they deserve a more nuanced conversation than they usually get.

## What is a prophetic dream?

A prophetic dream — or a precognitive dream, as it's called in research — is a dream that appears to predict a future event, in a way that can't be explained by information the dreamer already had access to. It's that last part of the definition that matters most, and that's hardest to verify.

Prophetic dreams are reported across every culture and every era. Abraham Lincoln reportedly dreamed of his own funeral days before his assassination. Mark Twain dreamed of his brother's death in a steamboat accident. History is full of such stories. And today, large numbers of people report having had at least one dream that seemed to predict something.

## What does psychology say?

Mainstream psychology has traditionally been sceptical of prophetic dreams and explained them through three mechanisms.

The first is selective memory. We dream an enormous amount — estimates suggest three to five dream cycles a night, with up to fifteen or twenty dreams. We remember only a fraction. When something happens in life, it activates the memory of a dream that resembled it — while the hundreds of dreams that didn't land are forgotten. This is confirmation bias in its purest form.

The second is probabilistic thinking. We live repetitive lives with recognisable patterns. The brain is a pattern-recognition machine, and in dreams it can process probabilities based on available information — and occasionally "guess right." That isn't prophetic; it's statistical.

The third is memory distortion. After an event, we reconstruct memories in ways that make what we remember dreaming fit better with what actually happened than it originally did. This is well documented in cognitive psychology.

## Jung's perspective — the compensatory function of dreams

Carl Jung was more open to what he called synchronicity — meaningful coincidences that can't be explained by causality, but that are nonetheless real. He didn't dismiss prophetic dreams as pure illusion, but believed dreams sometimes communicated something from the collective unconscious that went beyond the known limits of the individual psyche.

Jung was careful about calling this supernatural. He saw it as a phenomenon we don't yet fully understand — neither psychologically nor scientifically. And he documented his own dreams and the dreams of patients where the overlap between dream and reality was precise enough to be difficult to dismiss.

He himself had dreamed of Europe drowning in blood and flames in the summer of 1913 — months before the First World War broke out.

## What research has actually found

Some researchers have taken prophetic dreams seriously as a subject of study. The results aren't clear-cut, but they aren't uninteresting either.

A study from the University of Hertfordshire (Richard Wiseman) found that people tend to experience their dreams as far more prophetic than they statistically are — and that the selectivity of memory is the primary explanation.

At the same time, research on REM sleep has shown that the brain processes an enormous amount of information while dreaming — including subtle signals from the body and surroundings that aren't consciously registered while awake. A dream about illness may, in some cases, mirror early physiological signals the brain has picked up but hasn't yet communicated to consciousness. That isn't mysticism — it's advanced biological data processing.

It's for cases like these — where the body knows something consciousness doesn't know yet — that the term "prophetic dream" might be most accurate, though not in a supernatural sense.

## Dreams of the dead that seem to "warn" of a death

One especially common category is dreams in which someone visits you shortly before they die — without you knowing they were sick or in danger. These dreams are very common in grief research and are reported across cultures.

One possible explanation: we often notice subtle changes in people close to us — in tone, behaviour, energy — that the brain registers and processes in dreams without our being consciously aware of it. The dream isn't prophetic in a strict sense, but it is accurate: it reflects information that exists, but hasn't become conscious.

Another explanation: these dreams happen, but we only remember them afterward — selective memory activated by the loss.

## What you can do with a dream that feels prophetic

Regardless of what you believe about the nature of prophetic dreams, there's some practical advice worth following.

Write them down. A [dream journal](/en/guides/dream-journal/) is the only tool that lets you actually test whether your dreams come true — because you document them before the event, not after. It's the only way to counter the selectivity of memory.

Take them seriously as information — not as fate. Even if a dream mirrors a real signal you weren't consciously aware of, that doesn't mean the outcome is fixed. It means something in you knows something you haven't consciously worked through yet.

Avoid acting impulsively on a single dream. A recurring dream with a clear theme deserves more attention than one strong, isolated dream.

Ask yourself: what would I do if this were true? The answer can offer valuable information about what you actually want and fear.

## Signals worth examining, not fate to fear

Prophetic dreams are a real phenomenon in the sense that huge numbers of people experience them, and that they lodge themselves in memory in a distinctive way. What they actually are is less clear:

- Selective memory and confirmation bias explain many of them
- The brain's ability to process subtle signals we aren't consciously aware of explains some of them
- Jung's concept of synchronicity offers a non-supernatural but non-mechanistic framework for the unexplained cases
- There's no scientific consensus that dreams can literally see the future

What is certain is that these dreams deserve to be taken seriously — not as warnings to fear, but as signals worth examining. Start by writing them down, and see what the patterns reveal over time.

## Sources and further reading

- G. William Domhoff, *The Scientific Study of Dreams* (2003) — the continuity hypothesis, and why dreams that look like warnings usually mirror something you already knew.
- Carl Gustav Jung, *Man and His Symbols* (1964) — the compensatory function of dreams, which explains the experience of being "told" something.
- Artemidorus of Daldis, *Oneirocritica* (c. 150–200 AD) — the oldest surviving systematic dream book, and the origin of the prophetic way of reading dreams.
- Calvin Hall & Robert Van de Castle, *The Content Analysis of Dreams* (1966) — how common most dream motifs actually are, and what that means for the odds of a "hit."
