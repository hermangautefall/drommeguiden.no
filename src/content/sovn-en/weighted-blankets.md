Weighted blankets have gone from a specialist tool used in occupational therapy to one of the most talked-about sleep products on the market. And for once, the hype is at least partly deserved.

Just not for everyone, and not always for the reasons people assume.

## What a weighted blanket is

A weighted blanket is filled with a heavy material, usually glass beads or plastic pellets, that applies even pressure across the body. Weights vary widely, generally well above what a regular duvet weighs.

That even pressure is known as Deep Pressure Stimulation (DPS), and it's where the documented effect comes from.

## What the evidence shows

DPS is well documented in occupational therapy and sensory integration research. The pressure activates the parasympathetic nervous system, the same system triggered by a hug, a massage, or holding a baby. Cortisol drops; serotonin and oxytocin rise.

Studies on weighted blankets specifically show:

Reduced bedtime anxiety and faster sleep onset in people with generalised anxiety.

Better sleep quality and fewer nighttime awakenings in children and adults with ADHD and autism — this is the best-documented user group.

Reduced self-reported restlessness and anxiety in psychiatric inpatients.

Effect sizes are moderate, not dramatic. Weighted blankets aren't a cure-all, but for the right users the effect is real.

## Who it actually helps

Weighted blankets tend to work best for:

**Anxiety and racing thoughts at bedtime.** The pressure provides a physical anchor that many people find calming and sleep-promoting.

**ADHD.** The steady sensory input appears to reduce nighttime overactivation of the nervous system.

**Autism.** DPS is a well-documented method for reducing sensory overload and promoting relaxation.

**General restless sleep.** Many people without any diagnosis find the pressure produces a calming, "held" sensation.

It's likely to help less with:

**Sleep apnoea** — pressure across the chest could potentially be counterproductive for breathing problems.

**Claustrophobia** — some people find the weight feels confining and uncomfortable.

**People who sleep hot** — weighted blankets retain more heat than ordinary duvets and can make things worse for anyone who already runs warm at night.

## Getting the weight right

A common rule of thumb is that the blanket's weight should be roughly a tenth of body weight. But this is a starting point, not a fixed rule — try it and see. Some people prefer something lighter; others want more pressure. What matters most is that the weight is evenly distributed and doesn't feel uncomfortable or restrictive.

For children, stick to lower weights than for adults, and never use a weighted blanket for infants or very young children without professional guidance.

## What to look for

**Material.** Glass bead filling is superior to plastic pellets — more even weight distribution, quieter when you move, and better temperature regulation. The best blankets use glass beads sewn into pockets that keep them evenly distributed.

**Cover.** A removable, machine-washable cover is essential, since the cover collects sweat and needs regular washing. Cotton suits most people; cooler materials such as bamboo or Tencel work better for people who sleep hot.

**Stitching and construction.** Make sure the seams are solid — poorly made blankets can split and leak their filling.

## Weighted blankets and your dreams

An interesting pattern reported by many weighted blanket users: they experience deeper, more coherent REM sleep, which produces more vivid and structured dreams.

This is consistent with the mechanism — DPS reduces nighttime restlessness and fragmentation, meaning fewer awakenings out of REM sleep and better dream recall.

For anyone actively keeping a [dream journal](/en/guides/dream-journal/), a weighted blanket can be a useful complement — better sleep quality means more access to dream material.

## The verdict

Weighted blankets are documented to work, for the right people:

- Deep Pressure Stimulation activates the parasympathetic nervous system and reduces anxiety and restlessness
- Best documented for anxiety, ADHD, autism and general restless sleep
- A rough guide is about a tenth of body weight, but personal preference should guide the final choice
- Choose glass bead filling, a removable cotton cover, and solid construction
- Not well suited to sleep apnoea, claustrophobia, or people who sleep very hot
