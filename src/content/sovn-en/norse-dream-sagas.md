In Old Norse culture — the world of Viking-Age Scandinavia, roughly the 8th to 11th centuries, and the Icelandic society that grew out of it — dreams weren't interpreted so much as obeyed. A dream wasn't a symbol to analyze over breakfast. It was a message, a warning, a description of fate that demanded action.

Much of what we know about this comes from the Icelandic sagas — prose narratives about real and legendary events, written down roughly between 1200 and 1350, centuries after the events they describe are set in. The sagas' view of dreams is fundamentally different from the modern psychological perspective, and just as fascinating.

## The status of dreams in the Old Norse world

For the Norse, dreams fell into distinct categories with different levels of credibility and different sources:

**Draumar** — ordinary dreams, perhaps just noise from everyday life.

**Spádómar** — prophetic dreams, foretelling the future, believed to come from the Norns (the female beings who shaped fate) or other supernatural powers.

**Sendings** — dreams sent by enemies with magical intent, meant to harm or disturb.

**Visits from the dead** — dreams in which the deceased, including those thought to inhabit burial mounds, returned with messages.

Interpreting dreams — draumtyding — was a recognized skill, and those who mastered it were consulted much like other wise figures in the community.

## The warning dream in Njál's Saga

In Njál's Saga — widely regarded as the greatest of the Icelandic sagas — a man named Flosi dreams a dark, precisely prophetic dream the night before the burning of Bergthorshvol, where Njál and his family are killed by fire:

A man appears in a raging storm on the mountain Lómagnúpur, dressed in goatskin, carrying a staff. He calls out the names of every man who will die in the coming battle, one by one. When Flosi woke and told the dream, no one doubted it: this wasn't just a dream. It was an omen.

The saga uses dreams as structural warnings — they aren't incidental dream scenes, they're narrative signposts for what's coming, and the characters act accordingly. It's a function dreams serve in nearly all the great sagas.

## Egil's Saga and Egil's dream of insight

Egil Skallagrímsson — Iceland's foremost skald and one of saga literature's most complex characters — has a series of dreams in his saga that all turn out to be precisely prophetic. After his son Böðvar drowns, Egil dreams that his son visits him and speaks with him — a comforting, clarifying dream that Egil draws on to compose "Sonatorrek" ("The Loss of Sons"), one of Old Norse literature's most personal and profound works.

Here, the dream visit isn't just an omen — it's a source of understanding and creative material.

## The völva's dreams and prophecy

The völur — the Norse seeresses — practiced a form of ritual trance called seiðr, in which they entered altered states of consciousness to receive visions and dreams. It's possible this was a form of controlled hypnagogic or dream-adjacent state — the biologically real borderland between sleep and wakefulness where visions are especially accessible.

The Eddic poem Völuspá ("The Seeress's Prophecy") is the best-known Old Norse text of this kind, in which an awakened völva recounts the creation and destruction of the world. It opens with a consciousness summoned out of sleep. See the guide on [prophetic dreams](/en/guides/prophetic-dreams/) for what modern research says about dreams that seem to foresee the future.

## Draumkvæde — Norway's dream epic

Norwegian literature has its own unique dream work: Draumkvæde ("The Dream Ballad"), a medieval Norwegian poem of unknown origin in which a man named Olav Åsteson sleeps through the Christmas season — from Christmas Eve to Epiphany — and travels through an expansive dream world reminiscent of Dante's *Divine Comedy*.

Åsteson travels through purgatory, meets devils and the souls of the dead, and returns with a detailed report of what awaits on the other side. The poem was traditionally performed at wakes and funerals, and it blends Christian eschatology with an older Norse belief in the literal reality of dreams.

Draumkvæde is unique in northern European literature as a long narrative poem in which a dream is itself the entire event.

## What separates Old Norse dream belief from modern psychology

The fundamental divide is where dreams were thought to come from. Modern psychology places dreams inside the dreamer — they're the product of the brain processing the dreamer's own material. Old Norse belief placed many dreams outside the dreamer — coming from the Norns, from the dead, from gods, from enemies wielding magical power.

That isn't necessarily a better or worse view. It's a different one.

But it shares one insight with modern dream psychology: dreams are meaningful. They carry information worth listening to. And dismissing them as irrelevant is a kind of self-deception.

Jung wouldn't have disagreed with a medieval Norse dream-interpreter that dreams communicate something important. He would simply have located the source differently.

## A different source, the same core insight

Old Norse dream belief is one of the richest and most sophisticated dream cultures in European history:

- Dreams were omens, messages, and fate — not symbols for psychological analysis
- The sagas use dreams as structural devices: they always foreshadow something that happens
- The völur's seiðr was a ritualized dream-adjacent or hypnagogic state
- Draumkvæde is a unique Norwegian literary work in which a midwinter sleep is a journey to the realm of the dead
- What it shares with modern psychology: dreams are meaningful and carry information
