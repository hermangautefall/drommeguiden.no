A stressful meeting ends at 8 p.m. A few hours of news, scrolling, and shows. And then, at 11:30 p.m., we expect the body to simply switch off and fall asleep.

That's about as reasonable as driving on the highway at 70 mph and expecting the car to stop the instant you lift your foot off the gas.

A bedtime routine is your body and brain's wind-down protocol — a gradual shift from activation to relaxation that makes falling asleep easier and sleep quality better. It doesn't need to take long, and it doesn't need to be complicated.

## Why routines work

The brain is conditionable. Repeated actions performed in the same order, at roughly the same time, teach the brain to associate them with what comes next. Over time, a consistent bedtime routine becomes a sleep signal in itself — the body starts preparing for sleep from the very first step of the routine, long before your head hits the pillow.

It's the same mechanism that makes you hungry when you smell food, or sleepy when you sit down in the reading lamp you always use in the evening.

## The 7 steps

### Step 1: Dim the lights (60–90 minutes before bed)

Light intensity is the strongest regulator of your biological clock. Switch from overhead lighting to lamps. Use warmer light sources (under 3000K). Turn down screen brightness.

This isn't optional — it's biology. Melatonin production starts as light intensity drops. Every minute spent in dimmer light is a minute the sleep process has already begun.

### Step 2: Cut the screens (45–60 minutes before bed)

Blue light, a constant stream of new information, and the dopamine-driven pull of social media keep the brain in "seeking mode" — the exact opposite of sleep-readiness mode.

Put the phone down. Turn off the TV. Find something analog: a book, a magazine, knitting, drawing.

Can't cut screens entirely? Use night mode or a blue-light filter, lower the brightness as far as it goes, and set a "do not disturb" boundary that blocks notifications.

### Step 3: Write down the unfinished (30–40 minutes before)

The Zeigarnik effect is real: the brain actively holds on to unfinished thoughts and tasks so they won't be forgotten. At night, they resurface as worry.

Take 5–10 minutes to write down tomorrow's two most important tasks and any active worries. Not to solve them — just to "park" them. Research shows this concretely reduces the time it takes to fall asleep.

One alternative is a [dream journal](/en/guides/dream-journal/): writing down the evening's thoughts and, over time, reflections on your dreams.

### Step 4: A warm bath or shower (60–90 minutes before bed)

A warm bath or shower raises skin temperature. The body responds by increasing heat loss (cooling itself down). The temperature drop that follows triggers sleepiness — the same mechanism behind a cool bedroom.

Timing matters: take it 60–90 minutes before your intended bedtime so the temperature drop coincides with when you actually go to sleep.

### Step 5: Don't check the clock

Clock-checking in the middle of trying to fall asleep is one of the most common and most destructive habits: "It's 12:47. I only have 5 hours and 13 minutes left. I'm going to be exhausted tomorrow..."

Turn the clock away. Put the phone out of sight. Arousal from clock-checking is a well-documented phenomenon in insomnia research.

### Step 6: A relaxation technique (10–15 minutes)

Pick one technique you like and practice it consistently:

**4-7-8 breathing**: Inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 7, exhale for 8. Activates the vagus nerve and slows the nervous system.

**Progressive muscle relaxation**: Tense and release muscle groups systematically from your toes upward. Simple and effective.

**Body scan**: Move your attention gradually through the body. Shift focus from your thoughts to the physical.

**Visualization**: Picture a safe, calm place in detail — this activates the same visual structures that dreams use.

### Step 7: A consistent bedtime

The last and most important step isn't an activity — it's timing. Do the routine at roughly the same time every day. Varying by more than 30–45 minutes weakens the conditioning effect and disrupts the biological clock.

## What to avoid in your routine

News and social media in the last 45 minutes — both are designed to keep you engaged, not to relax you.

Demanding conversations, conflict, or difficult discussions. Save them for the next day.

Intense exercise. Gentle movement is fine; a hard workout isn't.

Checking email or work messages. It reactivates performance mode.

## The routine and your dreams

A good bedtime routine doesn't just make falling asleep easier — it directly improves dream quality. A calm wind-down produces more continuous sleep, which produces deeper and more memorable REM periods.

The writing step in step 3 is particularly relevant for [dream journaling](/en/guides/dream-journal/): thoughts noted in the evening create a bridge to the night's dreams, and the morning's dreams can be recorded in that same journal.

## Your wind-down protocol

A good bedtime routine is your brain's wind-down protocol:

1. Dim the lights 60–90 minutes before bed
2. Cut the screens 45–60 minutes before
3. Write down the unfinished — park your worries
4. A warm bath 60–90 minutes before (timing matters)
5. Don't check the clock
6. Practice one relaxation technique consistently
7. Do it at roughly the same time every night
